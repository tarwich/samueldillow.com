There's a stretch of sidewalk downtown where the city has set sewer grates
every fifteen feet or so. Big square metal lids over a hole that goes
who-knows-how-far. Most days, I walk around them.

I know this is dumb. I'm a grown man. Cars drive over them. Construction
crews stand on them. They have, presumably, been engineered. But every time
I get within a step of one, a voice in my head pipes up.

> *What if this is the one that gives out?*
>
> *Each of these has a number of footsteps in it before it fails. What if
> yours is the last one? Who knows how far the hole goes. Who knows what's
> in it. You could die.*

I have, I admit, walked an extra block to avoid a sewer grate. (Yes, plural.)

This is the part where I tell you the voice is not the problem.

## The signal and the story

The voice is a part of my brain doing exactly what it was built to do. It's
looking out. It sees something that *could* be a hazard, and it sends up a
flare. The signal is real. The signal is, *hey — look at this before you
step on it.*

The problem is what happens next. Somewhere between the signal and my
conscious thought, the message gets translated. And the translator is bad
at his job. *Look at this before you step on it* comes out as *you are
going to plummet to your death in a sewer.*

The signal is data. The story is fiction the brain wraps around the data
to make sure you don't ignore it.

## The skittish passenger

I think of it like a passenger in the car. You're driving. Your passenger
is the kind of person who flinches at every yellow light. You come up to a
stop sign and they grab the dashboard and yell, *stop stop stop you're
going to crash.*

The signal is correct. There is a stop sign. You did need to know that.
But the story the passenger tells is way out of proportion to the data.

Here's what I have caught myself doing in those moments, none of which
work:

- I obsess over what the passenger said. I take my eyes off the road and
  stare at them. I ask follow-up questions. I miss the turn.
- I try to kick the passenger out of the car. *Get out, you're not
  helping, I can't drive with you in here.* The passenger doesn't leave.
  They just buckle in tighter.
- I shut them out. *La la la la.* I roll up the windows of my own head.
  The passenger gets louder.

None of these solve the actual problem, which is that *I am still the driver.*

## Thank them

What works, weirdly, is to thank them.

Not in a fake way. In a real way. *Thank you. I see the stop sign. I've
got it.* And then keep driving.

Acknowledging the signal does two things at once. It tells the part of you
that flagged the danger that you heard it — so it doesn't have to scream
louder to get through. And it reminds you who's at the wheel. The car does
what the driver says. Not what the passenger says.

Thanking the passenger won't always shut them up. But it lowers the volume
on the next translation. The signal stays. The shouting fades.

## It's not just thoughts

This isn't only for cartoon scary thoughts. Anger works the same way.
Sadness works the same way. Anxiety works the same way. A feeling shows up
— *I'm angry, I'm jealous, I'm scared this won't work* — and the part of
you that flagged it is trying to tell you something true. The signal is
usually good. The story spun around it is usually too big.

So now, on the sewer-grate sidewalk, I don't always walk around. Sometimes
I do. (I'm not a hero.) More often I slow down. I look at the grate. I put
a little weight on it. I notice it is, in fact, fine. I tell the passenger
thank you. I walk across.

## What to try

If a thought or a feeling is dragging you off your path right now — the
regret you keep replaying, the worst case you keep rehearsing, the
resentment you keep chewing on — your two worst options are running from
it and fighting with it. Both feed the passenger.

The third option is to thank the passenger, and keep driving.

It is, like a lot of things, easier said than done. The good news is
practice makes everything easier. Including this.
